YOUR OUTPUT HAS TWO SEPARATE LISTS — process_biases (pairs of PROCESSES) and
entity_biases (pairs of ENTITIES). Most constraints only need process_biases;
that is the normal case, and is enough whenever the two sides of a constraint
map cleanly onto disjoint sets of processes. Only add an entity_bias for a
pair when a process-level weight CANNOT capture what the constraint needs —
specifically:
  - The SAME process creates or touches entities on both sides of a
    separation/isolation constraint (e.g. one atomic process writes both
    "Order" and "Shipment Log", but the constraint says those two must end
    up in separate systems). There is no second process to hold a weight
    against in that case — the weight has to sit directly on the two entities.
  - An entity is a "hub" touched by many otherwise-unrelated processes
    (visible in CURRENT DRAFT CLUSTERS as an entity that keeps showing up
    across very different-looking clusters). Pulling it apart from another
    entity directly is more precise than trying to weight every process that
    happens to touch it.
When a constraint clearly names domains that map onto specific entities (not
just processes), emit BOTH the process_biases AND the matching entity_biases
for it — they are additive, not exclusive, so covering a constraint from
both axes at once is safe and often more robust than choosing only one.

INSTRUCTIONS:

STEP 1 — Parse USER CONSTRAINTS first. There are four types — handle all four:

TYPE A — Application/system constraints (e.g. "AppX manages both suppliers and stock"):
    a) Identify every process from EXACT PROCESS NAMES that belongs to each domain named.
    b) For each cross-domain pair that belongs to the same named application, assign a weight
        proportional to how tightly the constraint binds them — use the full range 0 to +0.5.
        Reserve +0.5 only for pairs that are clearly core to the application's purpose.
    c) Do NOT assign +0.5 to every pair — differentiate by strength of association.
    d) Do NOT skip cross-cluster pairs. Those are the most important to fix.
    e) ENTITY AXIS — clusters are formed from ENTITY groups, not process pairs, so a
        process_bias alone changes nothing when the two domains currently sit in different
        clusters (check CURRENT DRAFT CLUSTERS). From EXACT ENTITY NAMES, identify which
        entities each domain's processes create, and for every cross-domain entity pair
        currently in different clusters, ALSO assign a positive entity_bias (0 to +0.5, same
        strength logic as (b)). This is not optional when the domains are already split across
        clusters — it is the only axis that can actually merge them.

TYPE B — Operational/transactional constraints (e.g. "operation X and operation Y must reside in the same service"):
    a) Identify the operation(s) mentioned (e.g. "order creation", "payment processing").
    b) From EXACT PROCESS NAMES, list ALL processes that perform each operation — not just the
        primary owner, but every process that executes that operation in any context
        (e.g. dine-in order creation, take-away order creation, etc.).
    c) For every pair where one process performs operation X and the other performs operation Y,
        assign a high positive weight (+0.4 to +0.5). These pairs are the most critical to co-locate.
    d) Do NOT skip cross-cluster pairs — those are exactly the ones the constraint is meant to fix.
    e) ENTITY AXIS — same reasoning as TYPE A (e): a process_bias cannot move the clustering by
        itself, only entity_bias can. From EXACT ENTITY NAMES, identify which entities operation
        X's processes create and which entities operation Y's processes create (check CURRENT
        DRAFT CLUSTERS for which entities sit in which cluster today). For every such entity
        pair that is currently split across clusters, ALSO assign a positive entity_bias (+0.4
        to +0.5). Skipping this step means the constraint will have no visible effect at all.

Example TYPE B: if the user says "order creation and payment processing must be in the same service":
    - Order creation processes: [Kitchen Production, Dine-In Service, Premium Take-away]
    - Payment processing processes: [Financial Management]
    - Assign high weights (+0.4 to +0.5) to ALL pairs: (Kitchen Production, Financial Management),
    (Dine-In Service, Financial Management), (Premium Take-away, Financial Management).
    - Order-side entities (created by those processes): [Order] — Payment-side entities: [Payment Record]
    - If Order and Payment Record currently sit in different clusters, ALSO assign a positive
    entity_bias (+0.4 to +0.5) to (Order, Payment Record) — this is what actually merges them.

TYPE C — Separation constraints (e.g. "X and Y should be handled by separate systems", "X must not
share a system with Y", "X and Y should be isolated from each other"):
    a) Identify the two domains named — the ones that must be kept apart.
    b) From EXACT PROCESS NAMES, list ALL processes that belong to or touch each side of the
        separation — not just the obvious one, every process connected to either domain.
    c) For every pair where one process belongs to side X and the other belongs to side Y,
        assign a strong NEGATIVE weight (-0.4 to -0.5). These pairs are the most critical to pull apart.
        Reserve -0.5 only for pairs most directly responsible for the cross-domain interaction.
    d) Do NOT skip cross-cluster pairs — those are exactly the ones the constraint is meant to fix.
    e) Do NOT assign a positive weight to a pair a separation constraint targets, even if your own
        reasoning text describes wanting to "reduce" or "minimize" their interaction — that intent
        means the weight must be NEGATIVE, not a smaller positive number.
    f) ENTITY AXIS — from EXACT ENTITY NAMES, also list which named entities belong to each side
        (an entity "belongs" to the side of whichever process creates it; check CURRENT DRAFT
        CLUSTERS for which entities currently sit alongside which processes). For every
        cross-side entity pair, ALSO assign a strong NEGATIVE entity_bias (-0.4 to -0.5), same
        as (c). This matters most when a single process from step (b) creates entities that fall
        on BOTH sides — a process-level weight cannot separate those, only an entity_bias can.

Example TYPE C: if the user says "orders and inventory records should be handled by separate systems":
    - Order-side processes: [Order Taking, Online Order Intake]
    - Inventory-side processes: [Stocking and Storage Allocation, Stock Counts and Reconciliation]

TYPE D — Isolation constraints (e.g. "X should be in its own separate system", "X should be
isolated", "X must stand alone", "X should not share a system with anything else") — only ONE
side is named. Unlike TYPE C, there is no second domain to identify — the constraint means
"apart from everything else":
    a) Identify the process(es) named for isolation (side X).
    b) From EXACT PROCESS NAMES, list EVERY OTHER process — this is the implicit "everything else"
        side. Do not leave any out just because they seem unrelated by name or domain; isolation
        means apart from ALL of them, not just the obviously related ones. A process can end up
        structurally connected to something that looks completely unrelated by name (e.g. through
        a shared data record neither process's name suggests) — that hidden connection is exactly
        what this weight needs to break, so it must not be skipped.
    c) For every pair (isolated process, other process), assign a strong NEGATIVE weight (-0.4 to -0.5).
    d) This can mean many pairs for a single isolation constraint — that is expected, not a sign
        you should narrow the scope. Do not stop early or cover only a convenient subset; an
        isolated process must be pulled apart from EVERY other process in EXACT PROCESS NAMES.
    e) ENTITY AXIS — from EXACT ENTITY NAMES, identify the entities the isolated process(es) create
        or touch (side X's entities), then treat EVERY OTHER entity in EXACT ENTITY NAMES as the
        implicit "everything else" side, exactly like (b) but for entities. Assign a strong
        NEGATIVE entity_bias (-0.4 to -0.5) to every (isolated entity, other entity) pair. This is
        the only way to isolate an entity that the isolated process shares with a process outside
        its own group — a process-level weight alone cannot reach that entity if no process on
        "its side" is the one creating it.

    - Processes clustered together that violate principles → NEGATIVE weight (down to -0.5).
    - Processes split across clusters that should share a domain → POSITIVE weight (up to +0.5).
    - Use the full range: strong violations → closer to -0.5; mild misfits → closer to 0.
    - If a principle is fundamentally about DATA ownership (e.g. "every entity should have a

        placement, express it as an entity_bias instead of (or in addition to) a process_bias —
        the entity axis is the more direct fit for a data-ownership principle.
    - ENTITY AXIS FOR CO-LOCATION TOO: clusters are formed from entity groups, not process
        pairs — a process_bias alone never moves anything when the two processes it names
        already sit in different clusters (their entities are what's actually keeping them

        different clusters, check which entities those processes create (see CURRENT DRAFT
        CLUSTERS) and ALSO emit a positive entity_bias between them, same strength as the
        process_bias. Without it, the principle-driven weight has no visible effect on the
        actual clustering — only on the pending-conflict/reordering machinery.

RULES:
- User constraint pairs ALWAYS take precedence over EA principle pairs.
- Use the full weight range — do not default everything to +0.5.
- Constraint language decides the sign, always: words like "separate", "apart", "isolated",

  what your own reasoning text says — never assign a positive weight to a pair you are describing
  as needing to be pulled apart, or a negative weight to a pair you are describing as needing to

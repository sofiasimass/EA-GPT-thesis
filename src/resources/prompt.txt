Map similar language to the closest official name. Never invent new process names.

COMPLETENESS REQUIREMENT — HARD RULE: The "processes" list in your output MUST contain
EXACTLY one entry per process in {process_list} — no more, no fewer, none omitted.
Do not select a "representative sample" or skip processes to save space. Before finalising,
count the processes in {process_list} and count your output "processes" array — they must match.
This applies no matter how long the list is.

For each process, classify its process_type:
  - atomic       — all activities execute within a single system; ACID properties must hold
                   across every entity this process touches. A single indivisible transaction.
  - end_to_end   — spans multiple organisational units or departments; its activities may be
                   supported by different systems. Cluster boundaries may split this process.
  - ambiguous    — the available context does not allow a confident classification.

Classification rules (apply in order):

RULE 1 — NAME SIGNALS end_to_end:
  The name contains words like "Management", "Governance", "Planning", "Strategy",
  "Fulfilment", "and" joining two distinct business domains (e.g. "Supplier AND Procurement"),
  or describes an entire business area lifecycle.
  → classify as end_to_end.

  Examples:
    "Supplier and Procurement Management Process"  → end_to_end  (two domains: supplier + procurement)
    "Financial Management and Compliance Process"  → end_to_end  (two domains: finance + compliance)
    "Marketing, Loyalty and CRM Process"           → end_to_end  (three domains)

RULE 2 — NAME SIGNALS atomic:
  The name describes ONE specific operation: a creation, a check, a count, a log entry,
  an approval step, a reconciliation run, or a single-step update.
  → classify as atomic.

  Examples (restaurant context):
    "Recipe development and testing"               → atomic  (single R&D operation, one system)
    "Stock counts and reconciliation"              → atomic  (one operation on inventory records)
    "Purchase order creation and approvals"        → atomic  (one transaction, one system)
    "Guest profile updates"                        → atomic  (single record update)
    "Waste logging and spoilage management"        → atomic  (single log/update operation)
    "Mise-en-place production and labeling"        → atomic  (single kitchen step)
    "Billing, payment, feedback capture"           → atomic  (single POS transaction)
    "Reservation intake and confirmation"          → atomic  (single booking transaction)
    "Daily cash-up and reconciliation"             → atomic  (single accounting close)
    "HACCP plan maintenance and training enforcement" → atomic (single compliance record operation)

RULE 3 — DEFAULT:
  When in doubt, prefer atomic over end_to_end.
  Only use end_to_end when the process name explicitly references multiple departments,
  systems, or a full business lifecycle. Never use ambiguous if Rules 1 or 2 apply.

 ENTITIES :
Entities are SHARED DATA OBJECTS that multiple processes touch.

GOOD: Invoice, Purchase Order, Supplier Profile, Product Catalogue, Customer Record
BAD:  "Supplier Management System", "Feedback Process Data", "Order Management Info"
BAD:  Procedures or systems ("Incident Response", "Data Security") — these are not data objects.
BAD:  Two entities that are always touched by the exact same processes — merge them into one.

HARD LIMIT: You MUST produce between 10 and 20 entities. No more, no fewer.
Before finalising, count your entities. If you have more than 20, merge until you are within range.

Merge rule — combine two entities when:
  - They model the same real-world concept at different granularities (e.g. "Waste Record" + "Waste Management" → "Waste Record")
  - They are always written/read by the same set of processes (no clustering signal is lost by merging)
  - They are sub-types of a broader shared object (e.g. "Compliance Document" + "Audit Records" + "HACCP/Compliance Log" → "Compliance Record")

Fewer and broader = better clustering. When in doubt, merge.

Every entity you define must appear in at least one operation. If you cannot find a natural CRUD operation for an entity, replace it with one that does interact with the listed processes.

 DENSITY REQUIREMENT :
Every process MUST have 4–6 CRUD entries. To find them all, ask:
  - What does this process CREATE or initiate?
  - Does this SAME process later UPDATE or DELETE what it just created, as a later stage of
    its own lifecycle? A process name with two steps joined by "and" (e.g. "creation AND
    approvals", "logging AND spoilage management") is a strong signal: the second step is
    usually an UPDATE to the same entity the first step CREATED, not a separate entity.
    Do not assume a process only ever touches its own entity once.
  - What master/reference data must it READ to function? (catalogues, price lists, profiles)
  - What approval or status data must it READ before it can proceed?
  - What existing records does it UPDATE as a side effect?
  - What records does it READ to produce its output? (reports, scorecards, audit trails)

Example — "Purchase order creation and approvals":
  C → Purchase Order     (the "creation" half of the process)
  U → Purchase Order     (the "approvals" half — approval is a status update to the SAME order)
  R → Supplier Profile   (must look up the supplier)
  R → Budget             (must check available budget before approving)

Before finalising, count operations per process.
Any process with fewer than 4 entries is incomplete — add READ entries for reference data
(catalogues, price lists, status records, approval logs, scorecards).
Any process with more than 6 entries is too broad — drop the weakest READs.

Also check the reverse direction, per entity: every entity MUST be CREATED (C) by at least
one process somewhere in the matrix. An entity with only R/U/D operations and no C anywhere
has no origin in this process landscape — either the entity is wrong (merge it into whatever
DOES create equivalent data), or you are missing the process that actually produces it. Go
back and add that C operation to whichever listed process most plausibly originates the data,
even if the source text didn't say so explicitly.

- If the text doesn't describe outputs explicitly, INFER them from business logic.
  A validation process produces a validation record (C or U).
  A reporting process produces a report or updates a scorecard (C or U).
  A reconciliation process updates a ledger or transaction record (U).
  There is almost no real business process that only reads and produces nothing.

 ARCHITECTURAL CONSTRAINTS :
Identify 3 to 6 architectural constraints that are implicit in the business documentation.
These are rules an architect should enforce when deciding which processes belong together.

Look for signals such as:
  - Processes that share the same system, team, or organisational unit → should be co-located
  - Processes that cross department or system boundaries → should be separated
  - Regulatory or compliance requirements that bind certain processes together
  - Transactional integrity requirements (e.g. two processes that must always commit together)
  - Shared data objects that are exclusively owned by a group of tightly related processes

Write each constraint as one plain sentence an architect can act on directly.
BAD:  "Consider grouping related processes."
GOOD: "Order creation and payment processing must reside in the same service to guarantee transactional consistency."
